The problem

The biggest customers had outgrown group management

Plenty of teams and budgets, but no organization layer above the groups. No shared roles, no pooled billing, no centrally-governed templates. Every structural change had to route through Customer Success, the CTO, and the Director of Engineering, and a paused automation is lost revenue.

Decision

Nested inheritance was what survived

It wasn’t the obvious first choice. It’s what held up after the simpler options failed against real, multi-level org charts.

Permission modelWhy it broke, or survived
Ruled outFlat org-wide rolesEasiest to build, but it can’t bound reach. A regional lead could edit another region’s billing and templates.
Ruled outAccess-list per resourceAs flexible as it gets, but it doesn’t scale. Every new hire or template means re-granting across the tree.
Ruled outTwo levels only (org → team)Enterprises run region → county → office. A hard cap forced customers to flatten their real structure.
ShippedNested inheritanceReach is bounded to a branch, it scales as the tree grows, and it matches how the enterprise is organized.

Process

Never commit a structural change blind

Moving a team, deleting a payment method, removing a template scope, editing a locked design. Any of those can quietly break a running campaign.

The move dialog names each person who loses access and why, and who gains it under the new parent, so the person making the move sees the full blast radius in both directions.

That one rule ran through the entire project.
