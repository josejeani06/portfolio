Redesigning LettrLabs’ Map Workflow into an Audience Builder

I helped modernize a two-year-old geographic audience builder inside LettrLabs’ automation flow. The original ask was to fix map bugs, but the deeper issue was that customers often needed Customer Success just to create an automation. I shifted the work from “make the map better” to “help different users build audiences without getting blocked.”

Visit LettrLabs Open map prototype I built the prototype in Codex to make the interaction model tangible before engineering committed to implementation details.

The redesign shipped to production, removed seven audited blockers from the primary targeting path, reduced filtering wait time from roughly 3 minutes to 13–22 seconds, supported multiple audience-building mental models, and created the foundation for future paid data-filtering features.

The question that changed the project

Why do customers need Customer Success just to create an audience?

At first, the project looked like a bug-fix pass on an old map. But the more I looked at the flow, the less I believed bugs were the real problem. The map itself was not impossibly hard. The workflow around it was full of setup gates, dead clicks, missing controls, and repeated work.

My reframing was simple: we were not only designing map interactions. We were designing how people think about building an audience.

Business context

This was not a side screen. It sat inside the money path.

Around 95% of customers who create automations use the map. Three major enterprise customers made up roughly 75% of revenue, and they relied heavily on map-based targeting. They had also been asking for the experience to be updated, so the cost of leaving it alone was not just UX friction—it was revenue risk.

The map also mattered to future strategy. Today, filtering relied on USDA-backed data. A clearer audience-building model would make it easier to migrate toward LettrLabs-owned data later and introduce paid filtering, audience refinement, and ROI-focused targeting features.

Discovery

I wanted to understand whether the problem was really the map, or whether the map was simply where the friction became visible.

To answer that, I triangulated across sources instead of trusting one. I analyzed behavioral data in Hotjar—heatmaps, drop-offs, and rage clicks—and turned it into a journey map focused on where actions, validations, and edge cases actually broke down. Alongside that, I categorized the interview notes PM and CS had gathered into recurring patterns, ran a full UX audit, and used AI-assisted Playwright walkthroughs to replay the flow.

The goal was not to collect more observations. It was to compare bad paths against easier happy paths, and see whether they all pointed to the same structural problem.

Across every source, the same pattern surfaced: the issues looked different individually, but traced back to a handful of root causes.

So instead of fixing them one by one, I grouped them into seven recurring blockers that accounted for most of the friction—which reframed the project from fixing bugs to redesigning the audience-building workflow.

Problem behind the problem

The old experience treated everyone like one user.

The old system was optimized for one assumed behavior: open the map, draw a shape, create a radius, continue. But customers didn't arrive with one mental model—and the four that mattered each expected a different starting point.

Once I saw those differences, the direction became clearer: the map needed to support multiple ways to begin, then consolidate them into one audience.

Persona Summary

These personas did not need different products. They needed one audience-building workflow that could adapt to how they already thought about the task.

Concept shift

The first concept was not what we shipped.

The early direction was more task-first than map-first. I kept the concept visible because it shows how the framing changed before the final product direction solidified.

Concept 01

Start from the customer’s job, not the map control.

My first proposal gave people clear entry points before they had to manipulate a map: search for a place, define an area, reuse a saved area, or upload a CSV.

This was a direct response to the personas: not everyone arrived ready to draw, so the entry point had to match how they already thought about the task.

Concept 02

Simplify the entry pattern without losing flexibility.

The alternate version flattened the choices into a tighter list so the starting paths scanned faster. It made the options clearer, but it also pushed the map further out of the first impression.

That trade-off mattered. The team still wanted the map to feel central to the product, even if the product logic itself had expanded beyond freeform map interaction.

Why the concept changed

Product view

Start with the customer’s job.
Let CSV, saved areas, search, and map tools become equal entry points.
Reduce support dependency by matching how different personas already think.

Business view

Keep the map visible because it is central to the product experience.
Make the map feel more capable before users move into targeting details.
Preserve visual trust while still reducing the steps needed to build an audience.

Why PM agreed

PM research showed customers approached audience creation differently. A single rigid map-first path did not reflect the clustered personas they were seeing.

Why engineering agreed

Multiple entry points could still resolve into one audience model, which made the experience easier to reason about than separate one-off workflows.

Why CS agreed

Support questions were often about helping customers reach the audience they already had in mind, not about teaching them more map interaction.

The final compromise kept the map prominent, but the product logic shifted. We were no longer only improving a map screen. We were making the path to audience creation easier and less fragile while still respecting the business need for a strong map-led experience.

Design evolution

I used early concepts to make the interaction model tangible.

These explorations helped the team discuss the actual behavior of the product: how users start, how they add locations, how the map responds, and how selected areas become a usable audience.

Exploration 01

Make the first click useful before the audience exists.

The empty state exposed radius, polygon, and ZIP-based targeting immediately, so users did not have to hunt for the correct starting action after landing on a blank canvas.

That gave the map a clearer invitation: pick a mode, understand the job, and start building with feedback instead of guessing.

Exploration 02

Make radius creation feel immediate and measurable.

The radius concept focused on drag interaction, live feedback, and an estimated structure count so the action felt more like shaping an audience than operating a geometry tool.

Exploration 03

Turn polygon drawing into a guided mode.

Instead of asking people to infer the workflow, the concept added visible points, clear instructions, and a distinct finish action so the tool felt intentional rather than fragile.

Exploration 04

Support users who already know the place they want.

County and ZIP targeting acknowledged that many users did not need freeform drawing at all. They already had a region in mind and wanted direct selection paths that respected that mental model.

Exploration 05

Treat the map like a workspace, not a one-off drawing tool.

This version paired the map with a review table so selected areas could be inspected and edited in context instead of disappearing into an abstract setup step.

The point was to make location logic feel concrete. Users needed to trust what they had built before moving into campaign setup.

Decision making

The hardest parts were not UI decisions. They were trade-offs.

I tried to make the cost of each decision visible so PM, business, design, and engineering could align faster.

Question
Trade-off
Decision
Future

Should we start with tasks or the map?

Task-first was clearer for CSV and saved-area users.

We shipped map-first because business stakeholders believed visual context helped users trust the product.

I would still A/B test task-first.

Should clicking the map become the main interaction?

One-click selection was powerful, but combining radius, polygon, and place selection could overload the control.

We kept radius and polygon as primary CTAs and made place selection available through clearer map controls.

Test a combined select mode once we have more usage data.

Should we ship everything at once?

A full release would be cleaner, but the scope touched targeting, filtering, saved locations, CSV, exclusions, and QA.

We scoped the release so useful improvements could ship within the 6-week window instead of waiting for a larger all-at-once rollout.

Use faster feedback loops between phases.

AI prototype

The prototype changed how the team discussed the problem.

I built the first interactive prototype in Codex—the first time our team worked from an AI-assisted prototype instead of static Figma screens. Suddenly PM, business stakeholders, and engineering could react to a working model, and it changed how we discussed the problem.

This is where much of the validation happened. We pressure-tested the interaction, compared map APIs across three different sources to find the right one, and checked the UX stability of selecting and adding places directly in the prototype—which is what made the call to make clicking the easier, primary way to build an audience.

In feedback, 9 out of 9 engineers said the prototype was helpful—for researching map APIs, validating feasibility, understanding edge cases, and discussing implementation risks earlier.

Engineering partnership

I learned to design with constraints instead of designing around them.

Engineering evaluated at least three map APIs. My role was to clarify which user interactions mattered most: selecting ZIPs, cities, counties, and states; supporting radius and polygon; rendering large datasets; keeping feedback responsive; and making exclusions understandable.

I also learned how engineers read Figma differently. When flows were confusing, I did not only explain the screen. I explained the concept behind the screen, the customer need, and the trade-off. That made handoff feel more like a shared product conversation than a file review.

Design system
AI-READY SYSTEM

The prototype felt production-grade because it drew from a design system I built to be AI-ready.

Before designing new screens, I made consistency the foundation. I standardized every component on shared variables, then built a small Figma tool that flags when a layer is using an outdated variable—so the system could not silently drift as it grew. Once components were consistent and organized by category, I used AI to draft each one's definition: its purpose, and where it should and should not be used.

Then I made the system readable by machines, not just people. These layered docs act as an AI harness—an instruction set a model reads to write copy, follow recipes, and audit designs the same way a designer would. I also connected the system to code through Figma Code Connect and Storybook, so the final prototype could inherit the real components, states, and copy instead of approximating them.

Support signals

Ticket categories pointed to repeated reuse issues, ZIP/county targeting needs, and control problems around selecting only part of a larger area.

Projected

The first release removed the core blockers behind roughly 40 map tickets a week, so we projected support volume dropping toward 10–15 as saved maps, place-click, CSV, and exclusions shipped—plus higher self-adoption and easier conversion into paid data-filtering.

Future opportunities

One idea we intentionally postponed was separating audience creation from automation entirely.

Instead of forcing customers to begin inside an automation, audience building could become its own reusable experience.

Customers could define audiences once, save them, then reuse them across multiple automations.

Although it was not part of the six-week release, I still believe this architecture better supports long-term scalability because it narrows the user’s focus to a single job: building an audience.

Another is live insight on the map itself—a real structure or household count shown as users select an area. Once the targeting data is self-hosted instead of pulled from USDA-backed sources, the map could surface that number in real time, giving people faster feedback while building an audience instead of waiting on a separate filtering pass.

Reflection

I still think the task-first idea deserved a test, but the final compromise made sense.

My strongest rejected idea was a task-first entry point with three choices: use the map, upload a CSV, or use saved locations. I still believe it would have helped users who arrived with a clear job to do. But I also understood the concern: some customers needed to see the map first to orient themselves and trust the workflow.

If I had more time, I would build a faster feedback loop after each phase and measure time to first audience, map-related CS tickets, completion rate, filtering abandonment, saved-location reuse, and conversion from basic targeting into paid filtering. I would also A/B test the task-first entry point against the shipped map-first flow, scored per persona and use case, and read the results against the same heatmaps, drop-offs, and rage-click patterns I used in discovery—so the validation loop closes with the same behavioral evidence it opened with.

“We stopped optimizing for map interactions and started optimizing for how people think about building an audience.”
